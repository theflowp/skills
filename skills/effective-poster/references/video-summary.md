## Context (0:00)

Posters and flyers generated with ChatGPT have flooded the streets, to the point that the French lunchtime TV news covers them. A post by Basti Ui on the topic passed 7 million views and set off a wave of hostile comments. Two camps clash: those who find these visuals ugly and depressing, and those who see a magic tool that makes designers useless. The question asked: are the businesses using them shooting themselves in the foot, or have they found the miracle solution?

## Definition and signs (1:08)

Scope: images entirely created by ChatGPT and published with no retouching at all. Using generated elements that are then reworked (Photoshop, etc.) is out of scope.

Signs: busy composition (badges, banners, pictograms, textures, ornaments), no breathing room, the same three or four letterings that don't really exist and lack consistency, small dashes around titles, a yellowish cast, a pile of ideas with no art direction, typos. Yet they are recognized above all by a slightly unsettling overall impression, comparable to the uncanny valley.

## Do they work? (3:02)

Five criteria: grab attention within a few seconds, make the subject clear immediately, rank the information, read fast (contrast, nothing superfluous), be distinctive and memorable. Design serves the information and doesn't steal the show.

Three "fairly decent" AI posters put to the test:

- Spain–Argentina match: catches the eye and the subject is clear (football, teams, time in big type), but the small texts are scattered in no order, the whole is too busy and already seen.
- "Night Sundays" evening event: color mush, no reading order; only the title, the biggest element, saves comprehension.
- Pizzeria: bland and oversaturated, you hesitate between a sports trophy and a restaurant; fails every criterion.

## The redesign (5:05)

Critique of the "Night Sundays" poster: too much information, the place / date / time order needs rework, useless messages, two dates set in two different typefaces, an illustration halfway between watercolor and flat design.

A deliberately "low-tech" redo in PowerPoint:

1. a real photo of a night market as the background;
2. a big title in Impact, with a slightly dated drop shadow, top center;
3. place, date then time in Comic Sans, in a small box;
4. a badge summing up all the scattered text (market, activities for adults and kids, friendly atmosphere);
5. the town logo at the bottom and a short friendly message.

Result: less flashy, but a clear subject, ranked information, fast reading.

A more graphic variant: a display typeface, left alignment, a light gradient. Same structure, more professional look.

Last attempt: describing the poster to ChatGPT with the right rules (night market photo, title top left and left-aligned, legible factual info, nothing superfluous). The result is simpler and more legible, but the artificial feel remains.

## The real problem: the cost calculation (7:28)

Graphic design and communication are skills; without them, you settle for whatever your phone offers. The argument "ChatGPT costs €20 a month, a designer €50 to €200" forgets that a poster is an investment: €200 (or about 4 hours of your own time) for a clear, different poster that brings in 10 to 20 more attendees can return around €1,000. Conversely, a poster generated in 30 seconds, confusing and like all the others, saves nothing if the room stays empty.

Analogy: the shopkeeper who neglects their window saves time, but nobody walks in. The poster is only one lever among others (shopfront, cleanliness, products), but neglecting it remains a risk. A chalkboard and a marker ("coffee €5") did the job perfectly well. If you don't take care of your image, people don't pay attention to you in return.

## Food photos (10:58)

Fake photos of real dishes are frustrating: a dish is easy to photograph with a phone, near a window, and makes people want it. The generated photo makes a promise the plate served doesn't keep.

Message to small businesses: stand out through authenticity and do it yourself, even imperfectly; an A4 sheet with a photo stuck in the window is enough. Nobody is asking you to become a designer, only to communicate clearly. The warning aims to help shopkeepers, including those in real financial hardship, not to pile on. Counter-examples: menus with one poster per product, or four products per poster, where nothing makes sense.

## An erased identity (12:56)

Large chains are doing it too (hypermarkets, fast-food chains, a theme park): lack of money doesn't explain everything. Retouched photos are nothing new; the new problem is the erasure of identity. Quiz: posters from Intermarché, Carrefour, Leclerc (French supermarket chains) and one generated for the occasion are impossible to attribute.

## Why everything looks the same (13:50)

A study published in Patterns (Cell Press), built like a drawn game of telephone (Gartic Phone style): one AI generates an image, another describes it, a new image is generated, and so on for 100 iterations. A politician in front of a newspaper ends up as a plush living room with red curtains. Whatever the starting point, images converge toward a dozen generic visual clichés.

Conclusion: left to itself, AI doesn't move toward originality, it reverts to the mean. Same compositions, typefaces, colors, effects and visual codes; a consensual look, neither very beautiful nor very ugly, standardized.

## "Just give it references" (15:14)

Feeding in a creator's work and asking for the same thing "in this style" is plagiarism; in France, it can amount to infringement (*contrefaçon*). A tool making copying easy doesn't make it legal or ethical. Several brands have had to pull visuals copied this way; photographer Christian Mamoun found generated images on a brand's website very close to his own photos.

Testimonies from Christian Mamoun and Roxane Tyrbas: the tool is so simple it pushes people across the line, thinking nobody will notice; plagiarism has always existed but is changing scale; creators need to unite to get laws and sanctions.

## The legal grey zone (17:05)

Who owns a generated image? The answer given in the video: it depends on the tool. OpenAI's terms state that the customer keeps the rights to their inputs and outputs; other tools forbid commercial use. For a business or a town hall printing these posters, the risk is judged moderate today, for lack of case law and because a terms-of-service violation is hard to prove.

## "But it looks nice" (18:01)

A clip from Murphy Cooper (video creator, Canada): redoing your shop's visual identity with ChatGPT gives it the look of a cheap luxury knockoff, cheaper than handmade, precisely because the tool imitates the look of an ad agency. Professionals spot it instantly, the general public faster and faster, and many are already fed up. A particular heartbreak: old bistros with 80s–90s charm that replaced their menus with generated visuals.

A line coined by Lisa, who co-wrote the video, roughly: "AI looks good from afar, but it's far from good."

## Were posters better before? (20:04)

Amateur posters from before AI (compiled by the site "60 millions de graphistes", some from 2016) had the same flaws: garish colors, illegible, pixelated, or bland. One decisive difference though: they were all different. "Different-ugly" rather than "same-ugly". Their clumsiness tells a story: you can feel people having fun, the atmosphere of the place. The AI version says nothing about who made it or the atmosphere; a wine shop and an amateur football club end up with the same visual. Everything is leveled toward a low average.

AI may produce graphically "better" posters, but a poster is judged in context: on a wall of AI posters, the different poster jumps out, and vice versa. The point is to stand out, not to be an artist.

## An irresistible technology (22:29)

The early internet required a lot of interface design to connect the real world to the digital one. ChatGPT is the opposite: a simple text bar, then voice, no friction, a brief handled in 30 seconds without asking questions, a result often beyond expectations. Hence something magical and rewarding about it.

The IKEA effect: we place disproportionate value on what we partly created, a lever brands exploit by turning customers into co-creators. In a TV report, a person is delighted with "their" generated poster. At the individual level, that experience has value; as soon as you show the image to others, it's just one image among many, with no human trace.

Another mechanism: the slot machine. You rerun a generation, then another, for a random reward, as at a casino or with infinite scroll. The video mentions whole evenings spent rerunning "one last" generation.

## Consequences (24:39)

People who are uncomfortable with technology, usually excluded from new tools, no longer are: you talk, it produces. That explains how fast these images spread. The flip side: dependence on a "superpower" you can no longer let go of, credit limits that force hours of waiting, companies backing out of subscriptions that got too expensive, and likely price increases, as with Netflix or Spotify.

Open question: general contamination, or a return of handmade work, perhaps with a label? Signs of rejection: protest stickers on posters, AI-redone shopfronts and menus vandalized. Basti Ui says he no longer wants to walk into a shop with a generated shopfront.

## Replies to comments (28:26)

The visuals mocked on X as "smelling of AI" had been made in 2023 by a 3D artist, before these tools existed, and the ChatGPT-"improved" version a commenter offered was clearly worse. Stated position: neither pro-AI (economic, environmental and copyright problems) nor anti-AI (the tools improve some processes).

## Conclusion (31:36)

Many professions now compete directly with these tools, whose output looks good enough and almost free. OpenAI's advertising stages an idealized human life where ChatGPT helps everywhere; its content is now on TV, in the street, in restaurants, to the point of resembling the start of a content monopoly. The paradox: small businesses that no longer have money for a local designer are enriching a tech giant that standardizes the visual landscape.

Whether you use AI or not matters little: what matters is putting into what you make the things that make us human.
